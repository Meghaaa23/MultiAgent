§0 How to Use This Document in the Meeting

What this document is. Part A is a working Detailed Project Report (DPR) for a single EC broiler shed on your 1.5 acres: site layout, shed drawings, equipment list, cost estimate, batch economics, timeline, licences and risks. Part B is a numbered question bank organised in the same order a farm actually gets built and run — from choosing the company, to construction, to placing chicks, to settlement day.

How to open the conversation. Show him Part A first. Say: “This is my plan on paper for my 1.5 acres. I want you to attack it — tell me where the numbers are wrong for our area, and what you would do differently.” An experienced grower respects a person who has done homework, and he will correct real numbers far more openly than he would answer vague questions.

How to work through Part B. Don’t read all 150 questions like an interview. Pick the sections that matter as the conversation flows, tick them off, and write his answers on the ruled lines. The italic line under each question tells you why it matters and what a bad answer sounds like — read it silently, not aloud.

The 5 questions that matter most (if you get nothing else, get these)

  1. “Can I see your last three batch settlement sheets from the company?” — real income, on paper, not memory.
  2. “What did your shed and equipment actually cost, item by item — and what would you not spend on again?”
  3. “Show me a summer electricity bill and a winter one.” — EC economics live or die on power cost.
  4. “Who near us built an EC shed and regrets it — and exactly why?”
  5. “If I start on my 1.5 acres today, what capacity, which company, which equipment vendor — and will you walk my land with me?”

§1 Executive Summary

This report plans a single environmentally controlled (EC), tunnel-ventilated broiler shed of 350 ft × 45 ft (15,750 sq ft) on a 1.5-acre plot, growing ~24,000 broiler birds per batch under a contract (integration) agreement with a poultry company. Under this model the company owns the birds and supplies day-old chicks (DOC), feed, medicines, vaccines and technical supervision, and lifts the grown birds; the farmer invests in land, shed, equipment, power, water, litter and labour, and is paid a growing charge per kg of live weight produced, plus performance incentives linked to feed conversion (FCR) and mortality.

EC sheds cost roughly 2–2.5× an open-sided shed of the same capacity, but stock ~40–60% more birds per sq ft, run more batches per year, keep performance stable through summer, and typically earn a higher contract growing rate (~₹8–10/kg vs ~₹6.5–7.5/kg for open sheds — verify locally). The long-term logic of EC is: predictable bird performance → better FCR incentives → priority chick placement from the company → steadier annual income. The long-term risk of EC is: high fixed capital, total dependence on electricity, and total dependence on the integrator’s contract terms.

Indicative project cost is ₹1.25–1.45 crore including shed civil work, EC equipment, feeding/drinking systems, generator, borewell, electricals and support buildings, plus ~₹5–6 lakh working capital. Indicative net income before loan EMI is ₹10–20 lakh/year (Section 10 gives conservative / base / good scenarios), giving a payback of roughly 6–10 years. These economics are highly sensitive to three numbers that only local, current experience can confirm: the growing charge per kg, the number of batches the company actually places per year, and the summer electricity bill. Confirming those three numbers is the central purpose of the meeting this document supports.

Decision position

This DPR is a pre-decision planning document, not a commitment. The stated intent: build for long-term profit, start only if (a) a reputable integrator confirms in writing the growing rate and batch placement frequency, (b) at least two working EC farmers in the district independently confirm the economics, and (c) total project cost including working capital can be funded with at least 25–30% own margin.

§2 The Contract (Integration) Model — Who Provides What

Almost all organised broiler growing in India runs on the integration model. Understanding exactly where the boundary of responsibility lies — and where companies quietly shift costs onto farmers — is the single most important commercial homework before signing.

How the farmer is paid (typical structure — verify every element)

  • Base growing charge: ~₹8–10 per kg live weight for EC sheds (open sheds ~₹6.5–7.5). Some companies pay a flat rate; others pay on a performance grid.
  • Performance incentive: extra ₹0.5–2.0/kg for beating the standard FCR / production-efficiency targets; this is where good EC farmers make their real margin.
  • Minimum growing charge (MGC): some contracts guarantee a floor amount per kg or per bird even in a bad batch — a very valuable clause; ask if it exists locally.
  • Penalties/deductions: excess mortality beyond a slab (often >4–5%), condemned/underweight birds, feed shortage/spillage, weight shrinkage disputes at lifting.
  • Payment cycle: typically 7–15 days after final lifting; delays are a known industry complaint — ask farmers, not the company.

Integrators commonly operating in India (examples)

Suguna Foods, Venky’s (VH Group), Sneha Group, IB Group (ABIS), Skylark Hatcheries, Shanthi Poultry / SKM, Japfa Comfeed, CP (Charoen Pokphand), Baramati Agro, and strong regional integrators. Which of these actually places EC batches reliably in your district is a local fact — a core meeting question (Part B, Section 2).

Contract clauses that decide your fate — read before signing anything

  • Who bears loss from mass mortality due to power/equipment failure? (Usually the farmer — this is why generator + alarms are non-negotiable.)
  • What happens if lifting is delayed beyond ~42–45 days — who pays for extra feed days, and how is weight loss/shrinkage treated?
  • Can the company skip placements (leave your shed empty) in a low-demand season without compensation? How many batches/year is it obliged to place?
  • Exit/termination terms — notice period, and whether your shed spec locks you to one company’s equipment standards.

§3 Land & Site Assessment — 1.5 Acres

Available land: 1.5 acres = 65,340 sq ft ≈ 6,070 m². For drawing purposes this DPR assumes a rectangular plot of ~440 ft × 148.5 ft with the long axis running east–west; the layout in §4 must be adapted to the plot’s real shape, road access and prevailing wind.

What 1.5 acres realistically supports: one EC shed of 350 ft × 45 ft done properly, with correct clearances, support buildings, truck turning space and a sanitary disposal area. A second full-size shed would violate the recommended 50 ft+ inter-shed gap and crowd the fan discharge zone; treat expansion as a separate-land decision later (Part B, Section 15 asks the expert exactly this).

Site selection checklist (score your plot)

  • Water: assured 15,000–20,000 L/day in peak summer (drinking + evaporative pads + washing). Borewell yield test is mandatory before construction.
  • Power: 3-phase connection of ~25–35 kW; distance to nearest transformer strongly affects connection cost. Check whether poultry qualifies for the agricultural tariff in your state — the tariff class can swing running cost by lakhs per year.
  • Approach road: must take a 15–20 tonne feed truck and night-time lifting vehicles in all weather.
  • Distance norms (commonly advised — verify local rules): ≥500 m from village habitation and water bodies; ≥100 m from highways; ideally ≥1 km from other poultry farms (biosecurity); away from wetlands/migratory-bird areas.
  • Orientation & wind: long axis east–west so side walls face north/south (less solar load); fan end pointing away from houses and your own gate (odour, dust, noise).
  • Drainage & flood: shed plinth ~2 ft above finished ground; the site must not waterlog — EC pads + wet litter is a disaster combination.
  • Mobile network: the controller’s alarm auto-dialer must have signal — a real, frequently-forgotten criterion.

Ask the expert to physically walk your land

The highest-value outcome of this meeting is not answers on paper — it is getting him to visit your 1.5 acres and mark shed position, gate, and fan direction with his own experience of local wind, rain and disease history. Part B, Section 14 is written for that walk.

Vendor rules of thumb (test these against his experience — Part B §5)

  • Buy fans/pads/controller from a vendor with a service technician stationed within a few hours — a controller failure with full-grown birds in May is measured in dead birds per hour.
  • Insist on spares on site: 1 spare fan motor, belts, pump, sensors, controller relay card.
  • Ask which brands the local integrator’s supervisors already know how to service — compatibility with company field staff matters more than a fancy import.

Read this table honestly

The spread between scenarios is enormous — that is the true nature of this business. EC contract farming is only a good long-term investment if your district’s real numbers sit at “Base” or better: growing charge ≥ ₹8.5/kg with incentives actually paid, ≥5.5 batches placed per year, and summer power under control. If local reality is the “Conservative” column, the project does not service a loan. The whole purpose of Part B, Section 12 is to find out which column your district lives in — from a man who has the settlement sheets to prove it.

Sensitivity: every ₹0.5/kg on the growing charge ≈ ₹1.4–1.6 lakh/year; every extra batch/year ≈ ₹2–4 lakh; every ₹0.5 lakh of summer electricity per batch ≈ ₹2.7 lakh/year. Litter sale income is real but variable — many farmers fund their litter purchase entirely from litter sale.

§15 Assumptions & Disclaimer

  • All financial figures are indicative planning estimates for India, 2025–26, and vary significantly by state, district, integrator and vendor. They are stated to make the meeting concrete, not to promise outcomes.
  • Plot assumed roughly rectangular (~440 × 148.5 ft) with road access on the short (west) side; the layout must be re-drawn for the real plot before any construction.
  • Capacity assumes ~0.65 sq ft/bird EC stocking; the integrator’s standard may differ and governs.
  • Scenario economics assume the company reliably places batches; no allowance made for a first “learning batch” performing below benchmarks (in practice, budget for it).
  • No revenue assumed from anything other than growing charges, incentives and litter sale.

B7 Water
